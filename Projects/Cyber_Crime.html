THE QUESTION

What happens when threats become data?

Cyber incidents are not isolated technical failures. They leave behind patterns — where attacks happen, which sectors are affected, how many people are exposed, and what happens after an incident is reported.

Between 2019 and 2024, the dataset captures reported incidents across different sectors, incident categories, response decisions, and levels of affected data subjects.

The challenge is not simply counting incidents. It is understanding the structure behind them.

Which sectors repeatedly appear in the data? Which incident types dominate? Are certain periods associated with larger numbers of affected individuals? And what decisions follow an incident once it has been reported?

01
The objective was to turn incident records into interpretable cyber-risk patterns.

Instead of treating every record independently, the analysis connects sector, incident category, affected subjects, response action, and time.

TAKEAWAYS

What this analysis actually uncovered.

Cybersecurity data becomes useful when frequency, impact, time and context are analyzed together.

01 Sector concentration matters.

Healthcare, finance and government repeatedly emerge as important areas of incident activity.

02 Common threats remain visible.

Phishing, unauthorized access and misdelivery appear prominently across the analyzed records.

03 Impact is not evenly distributed.

Incident frequency and affected-subject count measure different dimensions of cyber risk.

04 Time changes the picture.

Yearly and quarterly analysis reveals fluctuations that disappear inside aggregate totals.

05 Response behavior is another signal.

Post-incident decisions provide an additional perspective beyond the incident itself.

06 Visualization turns records into intelligence.

Combining distributions, rankings, comparisons and temporal analysis makes complex incident data easier to interpret.
